## Overview

This project focused on forecasting the potential revenue of a new ice cream shop.

The goal was to estimate expected sales and revenue under different business assumptions, helping the owner or decision-maker understand whether the business idea could be financially viable.

The project was developed as an academic business analytics exercise and combined demand estimation, seasonality thinking, revenue modeling, and scenario analysis.

---

## Business Context

Opening a new food or dessert shop involves uncertainty.

Before investing in rent, equipment, staff, marketing, and inventory, the business owner needs to estimate whether the expected customer demand can generate enough revenue.

For an ice cream shop, revenue can be strongly affected by:

- seasonality;
- weather;
- location;
- customer traffic;
- pricing;
- product mix;
- holidays and weekends;
- nearby competitors;
- marketing activity.

The project aimed to translate these uncertainties into a structured forecasting model.

---

## Problem

The main problem was to estimate future revenue before the business had stable historical sales data.

This created several analytical challenges:

- limited direct historical data;
- uncertain customer demand;
- strong seasonal variation;
- sensitivity to pricing assumptions;
- uncertainty around customer conversion rate;
- need for scenario-based decision-making.

Instead of producing a single fixed number, the project aimed to build a flexible forecasting structure that could compare different assumptions.

---

## Objective

The main objective was to create a revenue forecasting model for a new ice cream shop.

The project aimed to answer questions such as:

- What level of daily or monthly revenue could the shop expect?
- How might revenue change across different seasons?
- What assumptions have the strongest effect on revenue?
- What would happen under optimistic, normal, and pessimistic scenarios?
- How can the forecast support business planning and investment decisions?

---

## Methodology

The project used a structured business forecasting approach.

At a high level, the methodology included:

1. defining the business assumptions;
2. estimating potential customer demand;
3. considering seasonality and monthly variation;
4. estimating average order value;
5. calculating expected revenue;
6. building alternative scenarios;
7. comparing results across assumptions;
8. interpreting the forecast for business decision-making.

The project emphasized decision support rather than purely statistical modeling.

---

## Forecasting Logic

The revenue forecast was based on a simple business logic:

> Revenue depends on the expected number of customers and the average value of each purchase.

The model considered how customer traffic and purchasing behavior could vary across time.

Key input assumptions included:

- expected number of customers;
- average order value;
- seasonal demand changes;
- monthly or weekly demand variation;
- possible growth or decline over time;
- business scenario assumptions.

This made the model easy to interpret and adjust.

---

## Scenario Analysis

A key part of the project was scenario analysis.

Instead of relying on one forecast, the project compared multiple possible outcomes.

Typical scenarios included:

- pessimistic scenario;
- realistic or baseline scenario;
- optimistic scenario.

This helped show how sensitive the business could be to changes in customer traffic, seasonality, and purchasing behavior.

Scenario analysis is useful because early-stage businesses rarely have enough data for highly reliable statistical forecasts.

---

## Key Outputs

The project produced several outputs:

- estimated revenue forecast;
- scenario-based revenue comparison;
- monthly or period-based revenue structure;
- assumptions table;
- interpretation of business feasibility;
- recommendations for decision-making.

The main value of the project was not only the final forecast number, but the structure that allowed decision-makers to test different assumptions.

---

## Business Interpretation

The project showed that revenue forecasting for a new shop should not be treated as a single prediction.

It should be treated as a planning tool.

The forecast can help a business owner understand:

- how much demand is needed to make the business viable;
- which assumptions are most important;
- whether pricing or volume has a stronger impact;
- which months may require additional marketing;
- when cost control may be necessary;
- what level of uncertainty should be expected.

---

## Limitations

This project was an academic forecasting exercise and should be interpreted as a decision-support model rather than a production-level financial plan.

Main limitations included:

- limited real sales data;
- reliance on assumptions;
- simplified demand modeling;
- limited external variables;
- no full cost, margin, or cash-flow modeling;
- no real-time update mechanism.

A stronger business model would require real sales data, cost structure, customer acquisition assumptions, and continuous forecast updates.

---

## Future Improvements

The project could be improved by adding:

- real point-of-sale data;
- cost and margin analysis;
- customer segmentation;
- weather data;
- holiday and weekend effects;
- marketing campaign impact;
- competitor analysis;
- break-even analysis;
- cash-flow forecasting.

These additions would make the forecast more useful for real business planning.
