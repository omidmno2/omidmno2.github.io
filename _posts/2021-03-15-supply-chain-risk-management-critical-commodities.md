## Overview

This project focused on supply chain risk management for critical commodities.

Critical commodities are products or materials whose disruption can create serious consequences for customers, companies, or society. Their supply chains require careful monitoring because shortages, delays, supplier failures, logistics disruptions, or demand shocks can quickly affect availability and operational continuity.

The project examined how supply chain risks can be identified, categorized, assessed, and managed through a structured decision-support approach.

---

## Business and Operations Context

Supply chains are exposed to many sources of risk.

These risks may come from suppliers, transportation networks, production capacity, demand uncertainty, political conditions, natural disasters, market volatility, or operational failures.

For critical commodities, these risks are especially important because the cost of disruption is high.

A delay or shortage may affect:

- customer access;
- production continuity;
- public welfare;
- service reliability;
- inventory availability;
- cost stability;
- long-term resilience.

The project approached supply chain risk management as both an operational and strategic problem.

---

## Problem

The main problem was to understand how organizations can manage supply chain risks when the products involved are highly sensitive or critical.

A traditional cost-focused supply chain may not be enough for these commodities. Decision-makers need to consider resilience, redundancy, response speed, supplier reliability, and risk exposure.

The key challenge is that supply chain risks are multi-dimensional and interconnected.

For example, reducing cost may increase dependency on one supplier. Increasing safety stock may reduce shortage risk but increase inventory cost. Diversifying suppliers may improve resilience but increase coordination complexity.

This makes supply chain risk management a decision-support problem rather than a simple logistics task.

---

## Objective

The objective of the project was to create a structured view of supply chain risks for critical commodities and identify possible mitigation strategies.

The project aimed to answer questions such as:

- What are the main risks affecting critical commodity supply chains?
- Which risks are more likely or more severe?
- How can risks be categorized in a practical way?
- What mitigation strategies can reduce vulnerability?
- How can decision-makers balance efficiency and resilience?
- How can supply chains become more robust against disruption?

---

## Methodology

The project followed a structured risk management approach.

At a high level, the methodology included:

1. defining the supply chain context;
2. identifying potential risk sources;
3. categorizing risks by type and origin;
4. assessing the potential impact of each risk;
5. considering likelihood and severity;
6. identifying mitigation strategies;
7. comparing prevention and response options;
8. interpreting the results for supply chain decision-making.

The goal was to support structured thinking around supply chain resilience.

---

## Risk Categories

The project considered several broad categories of supply chain risk.

### Supplier Risk

Supplier-related risks include supplier failure, quality issues, dependency on limited suppliers, contract instability, and lack of supplier flexibility.

For critical commodities, supplier dependency can be a major vulnerability.

### Logistics and Transportation Risk

Transportation delays, infrastructure problems, customs issues, route disruptions, and fuel-price volatility can all affect commodity availability.

These risks are especially important when lead times are long or when commodities depend on international supply routes.

### Demand Risk

Demand may change suddenly because of emergencies, seasonality, market panic, policy changes, or customer behavior.

Unexpected demand spikes can create shortages even when the supply side is stable.

### Inventory Risk

Inventory decisions involve a trade-off between availability and cost.

Too little inventory increases shortage risk. Too much inventory increases holding cost, waste, and obsolescence.

### External and Environmental Risk

Political instability, sanctions, natural disasters, pandemics, climate events, and regulatory changes can disrupt supply chains beyond the control of individual firms.

### Operational Risk

Operational risks include internal process failures, poor forecasting, weak coordination, data quality issues, and limited visibility across the supply chain.

## Mitigation Strategies

The project examined several strategies for reducing supply chain vulnerability.

These included:

- supplier diversification;
- safety stock planning;
- demand monitoring;
- scenario planning;
- alternative transportation routes;
- supplier evaluation and qualification;
- risk-based inventory policies;
- early warning indicators;
- stronger coordination between stakeholders;
- contingency planning.

The project emphasized that no single strategy solves all supply chain risks. Effective risk management usually requires a combination of preventive and reactive actions.

---

## Decision-Support Perspective

A key part of the project was framing supply chain risk management as a decision-support system.

Decision-makers need to understand not only which risks exist, but also how risks interact with business priorities.

For example:

- minimizing cost may reduce resilience;
- maximizing resilience may increase complexity;
- local sourcing may reduce lead-time risk but limit supplier options;
- global sourcing may reduce purchase cost but increase exposure to external disruption.

The project showed that supply chain decisions should be evaluated through multiple criteria, not only cost.

## Key Outputs

The project produced:

- a structured categorization of supply chain risks;
- a risk assessment logic;
- mitigation strategy mapping;
- interpretation of key vulnerabilities;

- a decision-support view of supply chain risk management.

The main output was a clearer understanding of how critical commodity supply chains can be assessed and improved.

## Business Interpretation

The project showed that supply chain resilience requires proactive planning.

For critical commodities, organizations should not wait for disruption to happen before acting. They need visibility, risk indicators, alternative plans, and clear responsibilities.

The project also emphasized that resilience is not free. It must be balanced with cost, efficiency, and feasibility.

A strong supply chain risk management approach helps decision-makers understand these trade-offs before a crisis occurs.

---

## Limitations

This was an academic operations management project.

Main limitations included:

- limited access to real operational data;
- no full simulation of disruption scenarios;
- no detailed cost optimization model;
- limited validation with real implementation outcomes;
- reliance on conceptual and qualitative risk assessment.

The project should be interpreted as a structured decision-support exercise rather than a full enterprise risk management system.

---

## Future Improvements

The project could be improved by adding:

- real supply chain data;
- supplier performance indicators;
- quantitative risk scoring;
- simulation of disruption scenarios;
- inventory optimization;
- lead-time variability analysis;
- cost-resilience trade-off modeling;
- dashboard-based risk monitoring;
- integration with procurement and logistics data.

These improvements would make the framework more applicable to real-world supply chain management.
