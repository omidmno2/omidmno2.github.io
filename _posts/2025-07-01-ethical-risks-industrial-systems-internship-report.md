## Overview

This research internship focused on the integration of ethical considerations into **Manufacturing System Design** during the industrialization phase of Industry 4.0 transformation.

The work explored how digital technologies such as collaborative robots, artificial intelligence, cyber-physical systems, and digital manufacturing tools can improve industrial performance while also creating new ethical risks for operators and stakeholders.

The main output of the internship was a structured methodological framework designed to help project managers and engineering teams identify, assess, and mitigate ethical risks during industrialization.

---

## Research Context

Industry 4.0 has introduced advanced digital technologies into industrial systems. These technologies can improve productivity, flexibility, traceability, and process control.

However, digital transformation also introduces ethical concerns, especially when technologies directly affect human operators.

Examples include:

- worker trust in automated systems;
- physical and cognitive ergonomics;
- job security;
- data privacy;
- role clarity;
- operator autonomy;
- inclusivity;
- human-machine collaboration;
- long-term sustainability.

These risks are especially important during the **industrialization phase**, when prototypes, technologies, and design decisions are transformed into real production systems.

---

## Problem

Manufacturing System Design provides structured methods for designing production systems, including layout, behavior, control, intelligence, and performance dimensions.

However, ethical considerations are often not explicitly integrated into these design workflows.

This creates a practical gap:

> Ethical risks are discussed at a conceptual level, but engineers and project managers often lack operational tools to integrate ethics into everyday industrial design decisions.

The internship addressed this gap by asking:

1. What ethical risks arise during industrialization under Industry 4.0?
2. How can these ethical risks be anticipated, assessed, and mitigated within the design process?

---

## Research Objective

The objective was to develop a practical framework for integrating ethics into industrial system design.

The framework needed to be:

- structured enough to support project managers;
- understandable for multidisciplinary teams;
- compatible with Manufacturing System Design workflows;
- focused on actionable ethical checkpoints;
- usable during early design and industrialization decisions;
- adaptable to Industry 4.0 technologies such as collaborative robots.

The goal was not to create an abstract ethics discussion, but to operationalize ethics as part of industrial decision-making.

---

## Case Study

The report used a simulated industrial case study called **Hydrolienne Manufacturing**.

The case represented a company moving from artisanal production of mini-hydroturbines toward a more scalable production system. The scenario involved the introduction of collaborative robots to support production scaling.

The simulated case was used to explore ethical risks such as:

- operator trust in cobots;
- fear of job displacement;
- cognitive overload from new interfaces;
- physical ergonomic risks;
- inclusivity concerns;
- data privacy concerns;
- safety and failure risks.

The case study was designed as a realistic scenario for testing the proposed framework, not as a claim about a real company implementation.

## Methodology

The research followed a qualitative and design-integrated methodology.

It combined:

- literature review on Industry 4.0 ethics;
- analysis of Manufacturing System Design frameworks;
- ethical risk categorization;
- development of mapping tools;
- simulated stakeholder workshops;
- scenario-based ethical analysis;
- design checkpoints;
- feedback and documentation mechanisms.

The methodology was built to connect academic ethical frameworks with practical industrial design activities.

---

## Ethical Integration Framework

The main contribution of the internship was a five-stage Ethical Integration Framework.

### Step 1: Identify and Map Ethical Risks and Stakeholders

The first step identifies ethical risks and affected stakeholders.

This includes mapping risks across different stakeholder groups, such as operators, engineers, managers, safety specialists, and society.

The goal is to make ethical risks visible before design decisions become fixed.

### Step 2: Embed Ethical Checkpoints into Manufacturing System Design

The second step embeds ethical checkpoints into the design process.

Instead of treating ethics as a separate review after implementation, this step introduces ethics directly into design activities such as layout planning, tooling, and human-robot collaboration.

### Step 3: Conduct Scenario Workshops and Mitigation Planning

The third step uses scenario-based workshops to test and refine ethical safeguards.

Participants discuss realistic situations, identify ethical tensions, and propose mitigation actions.

This helps transform abstract ethical risks into practical design decisions.

### Step 4: Implement Real-Time Ethical Feedback and Continuous Improvement

The fourth step introduces feedback mechanisms to monitor whether ethical safeguards work in practice.

Examples include operator trust feedback, ergonomic discomfort reporting, and regular ethical review sessions.

The purpose is to keep ethics iterative rather than static.

### Step 5: Document and Certify Ethical Compliance

The fifth step focuses on documentation and traceability.

An ethics logbook records ethical decisions, mitigation actions, workshop outputs, feedback, and design justifications.

This supports accountability, organizational learning, and future certification readiness.

## Tools Developed

The internship proposed several practical tools.

### Ethical Risk–MSD Mapping Table

This table links ethical risk categories with specific Manufacturing System Design steps.

It helps project teams identify where ethical issues may appear in the design process.

For example, layout planning may create ergonomic or inclusivity risks, while human-robot collaboration may create trust, safety, data privacy, or cognitive-load risks.

### Ethics Cards

Ethics Cards translate ethical risks into simple prompts and mitigation actions.

They are designed to support workshops, design reviews, and stakeholder discussions.

Their purpose is to make ethical reasoning accessible to non-specialists.

### Ethical Checkpoints

Ethical checkpoints are design-time questions used during engineering reviews.

They help teams ask questions such as:

- Can operators understand the next action of a cobot?
- Are workstations accessible to different body types?
- Is emergency access clearly reachable?
- Is worker-related data collection transparent?
- Are human and robot roles clearly defined?

### Ethics Logbook

The ethics logbook creates traceability across the design lifecycle.

It records ethical risks, decisions, actions, feedback, and justifications.

This helps transform ethics from a general principle into a documented design practice.

## Key Findings

The research showed that ethical risks in Industry 4.0 are not separate from performance or design.

They are embedded in design choices.

For example:

- workstation layout can affect physical strain and inclusion;
- cobot behavior can affect trust and psychological safety;
- data collection can affect privacy and autonomy;
- automation decisions can affect job meaning and role clarity;
- interface complexity can increase cognitive load.

The framework demonstrated how these risks can be anticipated during design rather than corrected only after implementation.

## Contribution

The main contribution of this internship report was the operationalization of ethics within Manufacturing System Design.

The work contributed by:

- translating ethical principles into practical design tools;
- connecting ethical risks with industrialization decisions;
- proposing a five-stage integration framework;
- developing mapping tables, ethics cards, checkpoints, and logbook logic;
- showing how ethics can be embedded in routine design reviews;
- linking human-centered design with Industry 4.0 transformation.

The report bridges the gap between high-level ethics guidelines and practical industrial engineering workflows.

---

## Limitations

The work should be interpreted as an academic and methodological contribution.

Main limitations include:

- the case study was simulated rather than a full industrial deployment;
- several workshop interactions and personas were narrative-based;
- the framework was tested in a controlled academic setting;
- the tools require further validation in real industrial environments;
- scalability across different industries and technologies still needs to be tested.

These limitations suggest that the framework is a strong starting point, but further empirical validation would be needed before industrial generalization.
