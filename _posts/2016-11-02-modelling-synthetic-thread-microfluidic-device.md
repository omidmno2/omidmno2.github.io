## Academic Status

This work was developed during my Bachelor’s thesis in **Textile Engineering – Textile Chemistry and Fiber Science** at **Amirkabir University of Technology**.

It later led to a conference paper presented at the **12th International Seminar on Polymer Science and Technology** in Tehran, Iran, in 2016.

**Conference paper title:** Modelling Synthetic Thread as a Microfluidic Device  
**Bachelor’s thesis title:** Measuring Velocity of Wicking in Threads Used in Microfluidic Devices  
**Field:** Textile Engineering  
**Institution:** Amirkabir University of Technology  
**Conference:** 12th International Seminar on Polymer Science and Technology  
**Year:** 2016

## Overview

This project studied whether synthetic polymer threads could function as low-cost microfluidic devices.

Conventional microfluidic systems often require specialized materials, controlled fabrication methods, and precise channel design. This project explored a simpler alternative: using the natural capillary spaces between filaments in a thread as microchannels for liquid transport.

The core idea was that a thread can guide liquid flow through capillary action without requiring an external pump. This makes thread-based microfluidic systems potentially useful for low-cost diagnostic, environmental, or analytical applications.

---

## Research Motivation

Microfluidic devices are widely used to manipulate very small volumes of fluids in channels with micrometer-scale dimensions.

They are useful in fields such as:

- chemical analysis;
- biomedical testing;
- diagnostics;
- drug delivery;
- environmental monitoring;
- lab-on-a-chip systems.

However, conventional microfluidic systems can be expensive, complex to fabricate, and difficult to produce or use in low-resource settings.

Threads and yarns offer a possible alternative because they are:

- low-cost;
- widely available;
- flexible;
- easy to handle;
- capable of transporting liquid through capillary channels;
- suitable for simple device concepts.

The project investigated whether synthetic polymer threads could be analytically and experimentally studied as microfluidic channels.

---

## Research Question

The main research question was:

> Can synthetic threads act as microfluidic devices, and can the flow of liquid through them be estimated using capillary-flow models?

The project also explored how thread structure and environmental conditions affect wicking behavior.

Key questions included:

- How fast does water rise through different thread structures?
- How does relative humidity affect capillary rise?
- How does filament cross-section influence water transport?
- How does the number of filaments affect wicking behavior?
- Can theoretical capillary-flow equations estimate the observed flow rate?

---

## Experimental Design

The project used synthetic polymer thread samples and measured the capillary rise of a colored water solution through the thread.

The experiments involved:

- preparing thread samples with different structural characteristics;
- measuring filament diameter and cross-section using microscopy;
- controlling environmental conditions such as temperature and humidity;
- observing the movement of dyed water through the thread;
- recording the height of liquid rise over time;
- comparing experimental results with theoretical predictions.

A custom experimental chamber was also designed to help control humidity and hold the thread during wicking measurements.

---

## Materials

The main samples were polyester-based synthetic threads.

The experiments considered properties such as:

- thread count;
- number of filaments;
- filament diameter;
- filament cross-section;
- thread structure;
- relative humidity of the environment.

The conference paper specifically examined PET thread samples with different filament cross-sections, including round and triangular geometries.

---

## Methodology

The methodology combined experimental measurement with analytical modeling.

### Experimental Measurement

A colored water solution was used to track liquid movement through the thread.

The rise height was measured over time under controlled environmental conditions. This allowed the project to compare how quickly water moved through different thread structures.

### Microscopic Characterization

Microscopy was used to observe filament cross-sections and estimate structural parameters of the threads.

This was important because capillary behavior depends strongly on the geometry of the spaces between filaments.

### Analytical Flow Estimation

The theoretical part of the project used capillary-flow concepts based on simplified yarn geometry.

The project considered the spaces between filaments as equivalent capillary channels. Using this assumption, the flow rate and rise behavior could be estimated and compared with experimental values.

The model was useful for identifying trends, although exact prediction required considering additional parameters such as humidity, filament cross-section, contact angle, and thread structure.

---

## Key Findings

The project showed that synthetic threads can behave as microfluidic devices because liquid can move through the capillary spaces between filaments.

Several important findings emerged:

- water rise through thread can be observed and measured experimentally;
- theoretical capillary models can approximate the trend of liquid rise;
- relative humidity affects the wicking behavior;
- filament cross-section influences capillary transport;
- thread structure and filament arrangement affect the difference between measured and theoretical values;
- exact prediction requires more detailed modeling than idealized yarn geometry alone.

In the conference paper, the calculated flow rate for a PET thread sample was reported to be within the range of microfluidic-device flow rates. However, the measured value differed from the theoretical estimate, likely because of simplified assumptions in the model and limitations in measurement sensitivity.

---

## Interpretation

The results suggest that thread-based microfluidics can be a practical and low-cost direction for simple fluid-transport applications.

However, the project also showed that modelling flow in real textile structures is more complex than modelling flow in ideal capillary tubes.

Important factors include:

- irregular filament packing;
- variation in contact angle;
- filament cross-section;
- environmental humidity;
- surface properties;
- number of filaments;
- measurement limitations.

Therefore, synthetic thread can be treated as a promising microfluidic medium, but accurate flow prediction requires more complete experimental and geometrical characterization.

---

## Contribution

The main contribution of this work was connecting textile engineering with microfluidic device concepts.

The project demonstrated that:

- synthetic polymer threads can be studied as capillary-based microfluidic structures;
- textile geometry can be used to estimate equivalent capillary dimensions;
- experimental wicking behavior can be compared with analytical flow models;
- thread structure and environmental conditions significantly affect fluid transport.

This work introduced me to experimental research, polymeric materials, microscopy, capillary-flow modeling, and the connection between material structure and functional behavior.

---

## Limitations

The project was an undergraduate thesis and conference-level study, so it should be interpreted as exploratory research.

Main limitations included:

- simplified assumptions about yarn geometry;
- limited number of thread samples;
- sensitivity to humidity and environmental conditions;
- limited precision in some measurement methods;
- incomplete modelling of contact angle variation and filament surface effects;
- need for more experimental repetitions and broader material comparison.

---

## Future Work

Future research could improve the work by:

- testing more polymer types and thread structures;
- measuring contact angle more systematically;
- using higher-resolution microscopy for capillary geometry;
- testing different liquids and viscosities;
- modelling non-ideal filament packing;
- integrating thread channels into simple diagnostic devices;
- comparing thread-based systems with paper-based and PDMS-based microfluidics.
